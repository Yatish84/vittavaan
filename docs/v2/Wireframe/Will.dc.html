Keeping it true

Vasant has a will. It describes a life he no longer has.

Written in 2011, when he was 55, still working, and owned a house in Nashik. Every substantive clause in it now refers to something that has changed hands, been sold, or does not exist.

The clause that now points at nothing
"All my immovable property to my daughter Ananya." He has no immovable property. He gave the last of it to Ananya in September 2027, while alive.

The Nashik house went in 2018. The Karjat plot was gifted, registered and mutated a year ago — the one asset the clause could still have caught. A bequest of property you no longer own is not void, it is simply empty: the executor reads it, finds nothing answering the description, and moves on. Ananya receives the plot because of the 2027 gift deed, not because of this will. The gift that emptied it →

If nothing changes

The 2011 will would be produced, found to dispose of nothing he owns, and his ₹37,70,000 would pass under the rules that apply when there is no will. For a Hindu man, that is an equal share to his widow and to each child — here, half to Nirmala and half to Ananya, regardless of what he intended. It is not a catastrophe. It is simply not his decision, and it takes his family months longer to reach.

The gift in September was the trigger. Any transfer, sale, birth, death or marriage in the household reopens this question, and the platform is the only thing that sees all of them.

Four things to fix that have nothing to do with the will
1Add a nominee to SBI 8802. Ten minutes at a branch, and it is the difference between a form and a court.
2Replace the deceased nominee on both mutual fund folios.
3Itemise and photograph the locker contents, and record who owns which pieces.
4Note that the Karjat plot is gone, so nobody spends a year looking for it.

Nominations are quicker, cheaper and more immediately useful than any will. They are also the reason families think they have done estate planning when they have not — a nominee is a receiver, not an owner.

Ananya has already received ₹38,40,000 from her father during his lifetime. Whether that is treated as her share, as a gift on top of it, or as something to balance against Nirmala's provision is exactly the sort of thing a will settles and silence does not.

Record his decisions

Six questions only Vasant can answer

Recorded in his words, with the date and his authorisation. The advocate drafts from these; nobody else in the household can enter or change them, and Ananya cannot see this page.

01Who receives what
Named shares, or a percentage of everything, or specific assets to specific people.
"Everything to Nirmala. She has less than half of what I have and no pension of her own. Ananya has already had the Karjat plot and does not need this. If Nirmala goes before me, then to Ananya."
Recorded
02A residuary clause
Who takes anything not specifically named, including things acquired after signing. The clause the 2011 will lacked.
"Nirmala, and after her Ananya. Same as everything else."
Recorded
03Executor, and an alternate
The person who collects the assets, pays what is owed and distributes. Suresh died in 2021 and no alternate was named.
"Ananya as executor — she handles all of this anyway. Manoj as alternate."
Noted for the advocate: Ananya would be executor and, in one branch, a beneficiary. That is permitted and common, but it should be stated in the will rather than discovered later.
Recorded
04The gift, addressed explicitly
Whether the Karjat plot counts towards Ananya's share, or sits outside it. Saying nothing is the option that causes arguments.
"The plot was a gift, given freely and completely. It is not an advance on anything. I am leaving her nothing further because she needs nothing further, not to punish her."
Recorded
05The locker, and personal effects
Small in value, disproportionately often the thing families fall out over.
"My father's watch to Rohan. The gold in the locker is Nirmala's — most of it was hers to begin with. Everything else, Nirmala decides."
Recorded
06Medical wishes, and who decides
Not part of a will, and not something this platform should hold. Raised here because it is the question families wish they had asked, and the advocate can point him to the right instrument.

The part the platform did not do

Every word of the will. Vittavaan produced a twelve-page brief from the ledger — assets, documents, nominations, the family tree, the 2027 gift deed and Vasant's recorded answers — so that ₹26,000 bought judgement instead of data entry. What came back is a document neither we nor any template could have written.

What makes a will hold
✓Two witnesses, neither a beneficiary
✓Witnesses likely to outlive the testator
✓All earlier wills expressly revoked
✓Reasons given for an unequal distribution
✓Medical evidence of capacity on the day
✓Nominations that agree with it

Six things, none of them expensive. Wills are rarely defeated on their contents; they are defeated on how they were made.

We deliberately do not hold the will itself. A digital copy in a wealth app is not the original, cannot be produced to a court in its place, and would give a false sense that the paper does not matter. It does.

Where the platform must stay out

Succession rules differ by religion and by community, and a will that is sound for Vasant may be wrong for the next family. Nothing here is a template. The product's contribution is the brief, the contradictions and the prompt — the document belongs to a lawyer, and the decisions to the person whose money it is.
